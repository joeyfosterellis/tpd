## 3. Reply one: what bhakti is (GENERATED from SOURCED snippets, 03:34Z)

The reply as sent, lightly reformatted, words unchanged:

> Bhakti is the Sanskrit word for devotion, and the root says more than the translation does.
>
> **The word.** It comes from the verb root *bhaj*, which means to share in, to belong to, to partake of, and to worship. So bhakti is not only feeling. It is a relationship: the devotee has a share in the god and the god has a share in the devotee. The wider senses are attachment, fondness, homage, faith, love.
>
> **The path.** The Bhagavad Gita is the first text to use bhakti as the name of a religious path, one of three. Jñāna is knowledge, karma is action, bhakti is devotion. The Bhagavata Purana develops it further. Bhakti is the path that does not require learning or ritual skill. It asks for love and attention, offered to a chosen form of the divine, and it is open to anyone.
>
> **The movement.** From about the 6th century, poet-saints in south India, the Vaishnava Alvars and the Shaiva Nayanars, sang bhakti in local languages rather than Sanskrit, and over the next thousand years it spread through the whole subcontinent. Mirabai, Kabir, Tulsidas and Chaitanya belong to it. Its forms are song, chant, kirtan, and the repeating of a name.
>
> **Where it touches your work.** The mantra songs in your archive, the Gayatri and Sarasvati tracks with their TPDs from 23 September, are bhakti in form: a sacred text sung, repeated, offered. And the root *bhaj*, to share in, sits close to your equation's claim that a thing carries how it became. In bhakti the devotee is changed by the relationship and does not return to 0.
>
> Not answered by these sources: how bhakti is practised specifically in Nepal. That is UNKNOWN from this search, though the Bagmati sandhyā and the bratabandha whisper in your Savitri TPD are the same tradition.

## 4. Reply two: the translations today (GENERATED from SOURCED snippets, 03:35Z)

The reply as sent, words unchanged:

> Two answers, because "translation" means two things here.
>
> **How scholars render it in English today.** There is a live argument, and it is about your equation's question: is bhakti a state or a movement?
>
> - **Devotion** is the standard rendering, and the one most dictionaries and most Hindus use. Scholars now often add "personal devotion" or "loving devotion".
> - **Participation** is Karen Pechilis's proposal, from the root *bhaj*, to share in. In The Embodiment of Bhakti she argues that "devotion" makes bhakti sound like a feeling you hold, while the texts describe something you do. Her phrase is "devotional participation".
> - **Sharing** is the reading that stays closest to the root. Bhakti, bhagavān, bhakta and bhajan all come from *bhaj*. The devotee, the god, the song and the act of devotion are one word family, all forms of sharing.
> - Hawley, Sharma and Novetzke's Bhakti and Power (2019) adds that bhakti, even when translated as personal, always produces public life: gatherings, singing, movements.
>
> So the modern English choice is between devotion (what you feel), participation (what you do), and sharing (what passes between). In your terms, devotion names a state, participation names the arrow.
>
> **The word in other languages today.**
>
> | Language | Word | Notes |
> |---|---|---|
> | Hindi, Nepali, Marathi | भक्ति bhakti | The Sanskrit word, unchanged, in daily use |
> | Tamil | பக்தி pakti | Same word in Tamil spelling |
> | Bengali | ভক্তি bhokti | Same word |
> | Chinese | 奉愛 fèng'ài | The main modern rendering, "offered love"; also 信愛 xìn'ài, 虔信 qiánxìn, 奉獻 fèngxiàn |
> | Chinese, phonetic | 巴克蒂 / 巴克提 bākèdì | Used where the word is kept as a name |
> | Japanese | 信愛 shin'ai, バクティ bakuti | Written form and phonetic form both used |
> | Spanish, Italian, French, German | devoción, devozione, dévotion, Hingabe | German Hingabe, "giving oneself over", is the closest to *bhaj* of the four |
>
> Not established from these sources: the Tibetan rendering. UNKNOWN from this search. Buddhist Tibetan uses mos gus for devotion to a teacher, but I did not find a page tying it to bhakti and will not claim it.
>
> **On Nepali.** No translation happens. The word you would hear at the Bagmati ghats is the same word Krishna uses in the Gita. Only the sound has moved.

The prose that followed the table, words unchanged:

> **Colours to check.** The sources give six Egyptian colour groups, not four as I would have guessed, so take this from them: *kem* black, *hedj* white, *desher* red, *wadj* green (the word also covers fresh, flourishing, papyrus), *khenet* yellow, and blue as *irtyu* or *khesbedj*, the latter being lapis. Egypt was the first culture to make a blue pigment, Egyptian blue. So Egypt counted six, Newton seven, China five.
>
> **Seven.** Written as seven strokes. No glyph of its own. The Egyptians had seven Hathors, seven sacred oils, seven bows; the number was full, but it was not the octave's seven.
>
> **One caution on the wedjat.** The famous story that the six parts of the eye are the fractions 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, 1/32, 1/64, summing to 63/64, with Thoth supplying the missing 1/64, dates from the 1920s. Scholarship in 2002 showed the grain-measure signs came first with plain administrative meanings and were only later read onto the eye. So it is a beautiful story about 0′ ≠ 0, and it is a story, not Egyptian doctrine. It happens to use the same fractions as the tangram pieces, ¼ and ⅛ and 1/16. That is coincidence, not lineage. Label it GENERATED if you use it.
>
> **The oldest ouroboros** is Egyptian too: on a gilded shrine from Tutankhamun's tomb, 14th century BCE, in the Enigmatic Book of the Netherworld, two serpents biting their tails around a figure that may be Ra and Osiris made one. The snake that eats itself is the ring; whether it is 0 or 0′ depends on whether you think the snake remembers.
