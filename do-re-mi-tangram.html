Sa Re Ga 上尺工, Do Re Mi

Seven states and the arrows between them. Each state has a Sanskrit name and a Chinese name, side by side, and an English one underneath: the English names are 0, where you start; Sanskrit and Chinese are 7, where this page is going. The arrows are what the theory is about. Tap a state to hear where it is. Tap an arrow to hear what happened.

Sound

Eight keys, three voices, one drone

Everything on this page makes a sound. Choose the voice it is made in. The drone is Sa, Pa, Sa, the three strings of a tanpura: the note that does not move while the others climb.

Voice

On a keyboard, the number keys 1 to 8 play the eight notes. Down is the reversal question: the same arrows run backwards. Play 0 and 0′ together and you hear why they share a name and are not the same note.

Diagram 1

The staircase

Each note is one step up. Count from zero and the eighth step, high Sa, lands on 7. It has the same name as the Sa you started on, but it is an octave higher. Same word, different state: that is 0′, not 0.

On each step, the Sanskrit svara and the Chinese gongche character at one size. Under it: the number counting from 0, the letter in C major, and the English name, small.

Diagram 2

0 → 7 → 0′: the spiral

Bend the staircase into a ring and the top step lands over the bottom one. High Sa sounds like low Sa, so the journey seems to end where it began. Look closer and it lands one level up. The second zero has climbed seven steps to get there.

The arrows carry the letter T. Each one is the step that turns one note into the next. Tap an arrow to hear the slide; tap a note to hear where it lands; and hear how the last one sounds like the first without being it.

The equation

These are not meanings. They are simply different observable states. 7 is no more special than 3.

The arrows are the important part

Each arrow T is whatever causes one state to become another: heat, breath, pressure, movement, time, language, learning, decay, experience. So the theory studies transformation, not the numbers.

Two questions every arrow asks

3 →T₄→ 4

What had to happen for state 3 to become state 4?

4 → 3

Can the transformation actually be reversed? Sometimes yes. Sometimes no.

The final 0′ is the key

The system eventually appears to return to where it started. But the second zero has a history. A piece of clay before and after firing cannot simply be returned to its original condition. A person after an experience may return to the same place but is not the same previous state. A breath can leave and another breath enter, but time has passed.

A thing is not only what it is. It is also how it became.

The numbersshow where something is.

The final zeroshows that returning is not the same as never having left.

The scale is the smallest case. It takes seven arrows to come round, and the note it lands on sounds like Sa because it is Sa, an octave up. The tangram below is the case you can hold in your hands.

The arrows

Hear the change, not the note

A tap on a state plays a note: where something is. A tap on an arrow plays the slide from one note into the next: what happened to it. The theory is in the second sound.

T · the arrow is the key element

3 → 4

Ma slides into Pa. Something pushed it: heat, breath, pressure, movement, time. What had to happen for state 3 to become state 4?

4 → 3

Pa slides back to Ma. It sounds like the reverse. Whether the thing that was pushed can actually be pushed back is the second question, and the sound does not answer it.

Every arrow on this page can be tapped: the labelled arrows on the spiral, the arrows in the chain above, and these eight. "Play the arrows only" plays the seven slides and the return without holding a single note.

Diagram 3

Seven pieces, one square

A tangram has seven pieces and a scale has seven notes. Line the pieces up in order and you have the scale. Fit them together and they make one square: the whole set, the octave, home. Take the same seven pieces and build something else, and that is a tune. Bigger piece, lower note, the way bigger drums are lower.

Tap any piece to hear its note and see its name. Every time the pieces go out and come back, the square gets another prime. It is the same square. It has also been a boat. Play the picture reads the pieces where they lie, left to right and top to bottom, so the square, the row and the boat are three tunes from one set of notes.

Diagram 4

Where the names came from

Four traditions landed on the same count. The Sanskrit names are the oldest by centuries; the Chinese names stand beside them at the same level on this page. The English names, Do Re Mi, are the ones most readers start from, and they come from a Latin hymn used by Guido of Arezzo in 11th-century Italy. The Chinese names are gongche notation, 工尺譜, seven characters used as note names since the Tang dynasty (618 to 907) and still sung in Kunqu opera; two of them, 凡 and 乙, sit a little sharp of Ma and a little flat of Ni. The seven boards come from China too, and this page gives each board a note.

Sanskrit svaras

    Gongche, 工尺譜

      English, from the Latin hymn

        Seven boards, 七巧板

          Guido's system had six notes. Si was added later, from Sancte Iohannes, then changed to Ti in English so each note starts with a different letter. Ut became Do because Do is easier to sing. The fractions are each board's share of the square.

          Words

          Words that sound like each

          Each name is also a syllable that other words already use. The syllable does not mean the state; the words it echoes are a way to remember it. Ni reminds of clay in Chinese: 泥 ní, mud, the clay before the kiln. That one was yours. The rest are listed the same way, and each row says where its words come from.           6 → 7, and 67

          Ni → Sa′ · the smallest arrow before the return

          The smallest push

          Ni to Sa is a half step, one of only two in the scale (the other is Ga to Ma). The arrow before the return is the shortest arrow there is. It takes almost nothing to come home, and it is still not the same home.

          Smallest to largest

          On the tangram Ni is a small triangle, one sixteenth of the square, and Sa′ is a large one, a quarter. On the wheel, violet has to cross magenta, the colour with no wavelength, to reach red. In gongche, 乙 becomes 仩.

          The words on 6

          泥 ní, clay: what can still be pressed. 逆 nì, going the other way: the 4 → 3 question, sitting on the last step. Ni then Sa is niśā, night, in Sanskrit: the return comes after dark. In Newari ni is two: the sixth state, counted in Kathmandu, is a pair.

          67

          六七 liù qī. Six is lucky in China because 六 liù sounds like 流 liú, flow; seven, 七 qī, is a breath away from 氣 qì, breath, and 起 qǐ, to rise, tones aside. Flow, breath, rise: three of your arrows, read off two digits. In Newari, khu nhe. Whether 67 means something else to you is not on this page yet.

          Diagram 5

          Seven boards of skill

          Tangram is the English name. In Chinese the puzzle is 七巧板, qīqiǎobǎn, three characters that say exactly what it is.

          七qīseven
          巧qiǎoskill, cleverness
          板bǎnboard
          1. 1079 to 1118
            Banquet tables

            Huang Bosi, a scholar of the Northern Song, designs a set of rectangular tables and a book of diagrams, the Yanji tu, showing how to push them together in different ways to seat guests. The pieces are furniture, but the game is already there.

          2. 1617
            Butterfly tables

            In the Ming dynasty Ge Shan publishes the Butterfly Table Diagrams: thirteen tables in six shapes of triangles and trapezoids, named because their angles look like wings.

          3. late 1700s
            Seven pieces

            A simpler set appears, cut from one square: two large triangles, one medium, two small, a square and a parallelogram. This is the puzzle as it is still made.

          4. 1813
            The first book

            The earliest surviving tangram book, Qiqiao tu hebi (Complete Tangram Diagrams), is published by Bi Wu Jushi with diagrams by Sang Xia Ke.

          5. 1815 to 1816
            Across the sea

            Captain M. Donnaldson is given a pair of the books, one of problems and one of solutions, when his ship Trader calls at Canton. The ship reaches Philadelphia in February 1816 and the first American tangram book is copied from them.

          6. 1817 to 1818
            The craze

            The first Western book appears in London in March 1817. Within two years tangram books are printed in England, France, Switzerland, Italy, the Netherlands, Denmark, Germany and the United States.

          7. 1848
            The word

            Thomas Hill's Geometrical Puzzle for the Young holds the first known use of the English word tangram. Where he got it is not settled: perhaps Tang, the dynasty, plus the Greek -gram, or an old English word for an oddly contrived thing.

          8. 1942
            Thirteen shapes

            Fu Traing Wang and Chuan-Chih Hsiung prove that the seven pieces can make exactly thirteen convex shapes with no dents: one triangle, six four-sided figures, two pentagons and four hexagons. Everything else, the boat included, has a dent somewhere.

          One more thing the pieces know about 0′. Six of them look the same in a mirror. The parallelogram does not, so some figures can only be built if you turn it over. Same piece, other side.

          Diagram 6

          Seven colours, and three that aren't there

          Red is the lowest-frequency light we can see, violet the highest; Sa is the lowest note, Ni the highest. Walk 0 → 6 and in both light and sound you are climbing in vibration. That rhyme is not an accident. When Isaac Newton split the spectrum in Opticks (1704) he had five colours and added two, orange and indigo, so that the count would match the seven notes of the scale. He then divided the spectrum by the same string lengths that give the notes, and he wrote that violet is red coming round again, the way a note comes round an octave up. Newton's rainbow is Diagram 2 in light.

          Band widths are Newton's: red, yellow and green are the whole tones, orange and indigo the two half tones, which is why the two colours he added are the two narrow ones. Newton started his scale on D. This page starts it on Sa. Tap a band to hear its note. The page's seven colours are paint, chosen to read well on a screen. The thin strip along the bottom of each band is the light itself: the wavelength shown, converted with the CIE 1931 standard observer to sRGB, clipped to what a screen can show and set to full brightness. A screen cannot make 700 nm red or 405 nm violet exactly; the strip is the nearest it has. Where the strip and the paint differ, the paint is the choice and the strip is the fact: at 610 nm the light is already the screen's own red, and 480 nm is a sky blue.

          Three colours are missing from the rainbow, and between them they are the whole equation. Black is no light at all: the centre of the wheel, nothing has happened yet, 0. White is all seven at once, the ring around the outside: the octave sounding together, the square with every piece in it. And past violet is ultraviolet, which no eye can see. Red doubled is 860 THz, real and invisible. Yet the wheel closes: violet comes back round to red through magenta, a colour with no wavelength that the brain invents to join the two ends. The colour that returns you to red was never in the rainbow at all. Magenta is 0′ ≠ 0.

          The whole visible spectrum, 380 to 750 nm, is a frequency ratio of just under 2 to 1. The eye sees about one octave. The ear hears ten.

          #ColourPantoneNoteLightChakraWhat it carries

          The Pantone column gives the nearest named Pantone for each of the nine, with Pantone's own published screen value beside it: the six basic inks of the coated graphics system (Red 032 C, Orange 021 C, Yellow C, Green C, Blue 072 C, Violet C), Black C, and, because the graphics system has no indigo and no white ink, the two from the fashion and textile system, 19-3215 TCX Indigo and 11-0601 TCX Bright White. Pantone colours are inks and dyes; the hex values are Pantone's approximations for a screen, and Pantone's indigo is a good deal duller than the light. Magenta 0′ has no ink because it has no wavelength. Tap a band or a wedge to hear its note; tap the black centre for silence and the white ring for all eight notes at once. The chakra pairings follow the common seven-chakra convention; the meanings are the ones the colours carry in Nepal and India, where these notes were first named. The rainbow on the chakras, red at the root and violet at the crown, is younger than it looks: it was set out in California in 1977 in Christopher Hills's Nuclear Evolution and spread from there. The older Indian text on the notes, the Nāradīya Śikṣā, gives each svara an animal rather than a colour: Sa the peacock, Re the bull, Ga the goat, Ma the heron, Pa the koel, Dha the horse, Ni the elephant. And China's oldest colour system counts five, not seven: 青 qīng blue-green, 赤 chì red, 黄 huáng yellow, 白 bái white, 黑 hēi black, one for each element. Seven notes, seven boards, seven colours, five colours. The count is a choice, and Newton made his to fit the music.

          The clay before the kiln

          Fired clay cannot go back. Play-Doh can. Press it and it stays pressed. Roll it back into a ball and it is a ball again, with every fingerprint still under the surface. Each press sounds the note under your finger, left to right, Sa to high Sa.
